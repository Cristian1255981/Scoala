1Introduction

What is the project life cycle?

WHATWhat it is

The project life cycle is the series of stages a project goes through, from the first idea to the finished result (PMI, 2017). Every project, big or small, goes through the same stages in the same order: initiation, planning, execution, monitoring & control and closure.

Strictly speaking, it's more of a framework than a full methodology like PRINCE2 or Scrum, but most methodologies are built on top of it.

WHYIts purpose

To give a project a clear structure: what needs doing, in what order, who is responsible and when to stop and check. It keeps time, cost and quality under control and makes sure nothing important, like testing or training, gets forgotten.

WHEREWhy use it in software or systems development?

  • Software projects are complex, and without stages they get out of control fast.
  • Lots of people have to agree: the client, users, developers, testers and IT support.
  • Clients want to know the cost and the delivery date before they pay.
  • The decision points mean a bad project can be stopped before too much money is wasted.

3Each stage · stage 1 of 5

Initiation: is it worth doing?

1Deciding if the project is worth doing at all.

What happens

  • Someone spots a problem or an opportunity
  • A feasibility study checks if it's possible
  • A business case compares the costs and the benefits
  • The sponsor makes a go / no-go decision

Who's involved

  • Sponsor or client
  • Project manager
  • Business analyst
  • Senior managers

What's produced

  • Business case
  • Feasibility report
  • Project brief (with the objectives)
  • Stakeholder list

Benefits

Benefits explain why the organisation should do the project. A software project could:

  • Save money
  • Increase profits
  • Improve customer service
  • Increase productivity
  • Help the business grow
  • Attract more customers
  • Increase market share
  • Reduce manual work

My bakery: more sales, less waste and no more lost phone orders.

Return on investment (ROI)

ROI compares how much the project costs with the money or benefits it could bring in. E.g. a company spends £10,000 on an online ordering system and expects an extra £20,000 in sales:

ROI =100%(£20,000 − £10,000) ÷ £10,000 × 100

Every £1 spent brings back £2.00, so it's worth doing.

Success criteria & SMART objectives

Success criteria explain how we'll know if the project has been successful, e.g. “The website must allow users to create an account and log in successfully.” The objectives should also be SMART, for example:

“Create a working login system and test it successfully by 30 November.”

  • SSpecificclear: a working login system
  • MMeasurablewe can check it: the tests pass
  • AAchievablerealistic for our team
  • RRelevantcustomers need an account to order
  • TTime-boundit has a deadline: 30 November

3Each stage · stage 2 of 5

Planning: what exactly are we making?

2Turning the idea into a detailed plan that everyone agrees on.

What happens

  • Agree the scope and the requirements
  • Break the product into parts (PBS), then into tasks
  • Make the schedule and the budget
  • Plan for risks, quality and communication
  • The client signs off the plan (the baseline)

Who's involved

  • Project manager
  • Business analyst
  • Team leaders
  • Client (signs off)
  • Finance

What's produced

  • Requirements specification
  • Product description & PBS
  • Project plan & Gantt chart
  • Budget
  • Risk register

Customer requirements

What the customer wants the software to do. Functional (F) = what the system must do. Non-functional (NF) = how well it should work. Try sorting them:

  1. Users can log in.
  2. The website should load within three seconds.
  3. Customers can add products to a basket.
  4. User passwords must be secure.
  5. The system calculates the total price.
  6. The system should be easy to use.
  7. The website should work on mobile phones.

0 / 7 sorted

Tip: a good requirement can be tested. “Fast” can't, “within three seconds” can.

Product description & PBS

A product description explains what we're going to create. Mine: “A website where Northgate Bakery customers order online and collect in the shop.”

A Product Breakdown Structure (PBS) breaks the final product into smaller parts, so it's easier to plan and manage:

Click & collect website

  1. 1Home pagemenu, opening times
  2. 2Login pagecreate an account, log in
  3. 3Ordering systembasket, total, pick-up time
  4. 4Databaseproducts, customers, orders
  5. 5Admin pagetoday's orders for the shop
  6. 6User guidefor the bakery staff

3Each stage · stage 3 of 5

Execution: building it properly

3The team builds the product. In software, this is where design, coding and testing happen.

What happens

  • Design, build and test the product
  • Give out tasks and hold team meetings
  • Work with suppliers and keep stakeholders updated
  • User acceptance testing near the end

Who's involved

  • Developers
  • Designers
  • Testers
  • Project manager
  • Suppliers
  • Users (for testing)

What's produced

  • The product (website, app…)
  • Test reports
  • Progress reports
  • User guide

Quality

Quality means making sure the final software is reliable, safe and meets the required standards.

ISO/IEC 25010: software quality (ISO, 2023)

Usability: can customers order without help? I'll watch 5 real customers try it. (The 2023 version calls this “interaction capability”.)

W3C standards help websites work correctly and follow recognised web standards. I'll check my HTML with the W3C validator and follow WCAG 2.2 (W3C, 2023).

A deliverable is something we produce during or at the end of the project. The dot shows the stage where it's made:

3Each stage · stage 4 of 5

Monitoring & control: keeping it on track

4Runs alongside execution: checking the project is going to plan and fixing problems early.

What happens

  • Compare progress with the plan (time, cost, quality)
  • Keep track of risks and issues
  • Change control: approve or reject changes
  • Take corrective action if we fall behind

Who's involved

  • Project manager
  • Sponsor / project board
  • Quality assurance
  • Team leaders

What's produced

  • Status reports
  • Issue log
  • Change log
  • Updated risk register

Issues

Issues are problems that happen during a project, for example:

  • The customer changes their requirements
  • A developer is absent
  • Software doesn't work as expected
  • We're behind schedule
  • Poor communication in the team
  • New laws or regulations

I monitor progress and take corrective action when problems happen. If development is behind schedule, I can:

  • Change the plan
  • Move resources
  • Cut less important features

My bakery: if payments (task E) fall behind, the launch moves too, because E is on the critical path. So I'd move a developer onto it, or leave gift cards for later.

Risks

A risk is something that might happen and cause a problem. If it does happen, it becomes an issue. Risks in software projects:

  • Going over budget
  • Missing a deadline
  • Losing important data
  • Cybersecurity problems
  • Key staff leaving
  • Software failing testing
  • Requirements changing
Risk
A developer may be absent.
Impact
The project could be delayed.
Action
Share the project files and documentation, so another team member can carry on with the work.

The risk management cycle

  1. 1 IdentifyWhat could go wrong?
  2. 2 AssessHow likely is it, and how serious would it be?
  3. 3 DecideAccept it, avoid it or make a contingency plan
  4. 4 MonitorKeep checking it during the project
  5. 5 Take actionIf it happens, respond to the issue

  • Users
  • IT support team
  • Project team

What's produced

  • End project report
  • Lessons learned report
  • Training materials

4Key features

How it works: key features

  • Stages in a fixed orderEach stage only starts when the one before it has been approved.
  • Stage gatesA go / no-go decision between stages. PRINCE2 calls these stage boundaries (AXELOS, 2017).
  • Deliverables at every stageEach stage produces something that can be checked and signed off.
  • A baseline planTime, cost and quality are agreed up front and used to measure progress.
  • Monitored all the way throughProgress is checked during the project, not just at the end.
  • TemporaryA clear start and a clear end, then the team moves on.

56Advantages & disadvantages

Advantages and disadvantages

+Advantages and why each one helps me as the project manager

Clear structure

Everyone knows which stage we're in, what comes next and who is responsible for what.

Why it helps meI can plan stage by stage and explain progress to the client in a simple way.

Control of time and cost

The baseline plan (Gantt chart and budget) is checked at every interim review.

Why it helps meI can spot early if we're behind or over budget, and fix it before it's too late.

Bad projects get stopped early

At every stage gate the project can be approved, changed or stopped.

Why it helps meI won't waste the client's money on something that clearly isn't going to work.

−Disadvantages and how each one could affect my project

Hard to change later

The later a change is made, the more it costs (PMI, 2017).

How it could affect my projectIf the owner changes their mind halfway through, it could delay the launch and push the cost up.

Lots of paperwork

Business case, plans, logs, reports and sign-offs all take time to write.

How it could affect my projectWith a team of four, writing documents could take time away from building the website.

Users see it late

Users are mostly involved at the start and at the end, not much in the middle.

How it could affect my projectIf I misunderstood a requirement, I might only find out at acceptance testing, when fixing it is expensive.

7Suitable projects

What projects is it good for?

Best for projects where…

  • the goal and the requirements are clear and won't change much
  • the deadline and the budget are fixed
  • lots of people need to approve things
  • mistakes are expensive or dangerous (safety, the law, public money)

For example: installing a network, moving a company to a new system, or hospital and government IT.

  • Not ideal when the requirements keep changing, like a new app where you only find out what users want as you go. Agile suits that better (Beck et al., 2001).

Real-life example: NASA's Mars 2020 mission

NASA runs every mission through a formal life cycle, from Pre-Phase A (concept) to Phase F (closeout), with a Key Decision Point before each phase (NASA, 2016). The Perseverance rover launched on 30 July 2020 and landed on Mars on 18 February 2021 (NASA, no date).

Why it suits this project

  • Fixed deadline: Earth and Mars only line up for a launch about every 26 months. Miss it and you wait over two years.
  • No second chance: you can't repair a rover on Mars, so everything is designed, built and tested in order and checked at each gate.
  • Huge budget, many teams: formal approvals keep control of the money and the people.

8Link to my own project

Using it on my own project

It's the project from the stage slides: a click & collect website for Northgate Bakery, a small local bakery (made up for this unit), with 8 weeks, a £9,900 budget and a team of four.

  1. 1InitiationMeet the owner, write the business case (lost phone orders, queues, waste, ROI) and set SMART objectives. The owner says go.
  2. 2PlanningAgree the requirements, break the site into parts (PBS), then make the Gantt chart, budget and risk register and get the plan signed off.
  3. 3ExecutionDesign, build and test the website, check the quality against ISO/IEC 25010 and WCAG, and show the owner a demo every week.
  4. 4Monitoring & controlReviews in weeks 3 and 6 against the Gantt chart and budget. Problems go in the issue log; new ideas, like loyalty points, go through change control.
  5. 5Closure10 real test orders, check the success criteria, train the staff, hand over the user guide, get it signed off and write the lessons learned.

Would it work well for my project?

Mostly, yes.

  • The deadline (before Christmas) and the budget are fixed
  • The requirements are fairly clear from the start
  • The owner wants to approve things at each step
  • The owner might change their mind once they see the design

So I'd keep the life cycle, but show the owner a clickable prototype during planning to get feedback early. That's a bit of Agile inside it.

9Conclusion

Conclusion

The project life cycle splits a project into five stages in a fixed order, initiation, planning, execution, monitoring & control and closure, with a decision point between them. Each stage has its own jobs and tools, from the business case to the sign-off, and together they keep a project under control from the first idea to the handover.

+Main strengths

  • A clear structure that everyone understands
  • Good control of time, cost and quality
  • Clear deliverables and sign-offs
  • Bad projects can be stopped early

−Main limitations

  • Changes are hard and expensive later on
  • A lot of paperwork
  • Users see the result quite late
  • It's only as good as the plan

When I think it should be used

When the goal is clear, the deadline and budget are fixed and approvals or safety matter, like my bakery website or a NASA mission. When the requirements will keep changing, I'd use Agile instead, or mix the two.

my opinion: the more you know at the start, the better it works
