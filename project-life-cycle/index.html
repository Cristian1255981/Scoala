1Introduction

What is the project life cycle?

WHATWhat it is

The project life cycle is the series of stages a project goes through, from the first idea to the finished result (PMI, 2017). Every project, big or small, goes through the same stages in the same order: initiation, planning, execution, monitoring & control and closure.

Strictly speaking, it's more of a framework than a full methodology like PRINCE2 or Scrum, but most methodologies are built on top of it.

WHYIts purpose

To give a project a clear structure: what needs doing, in what order, who is responsible and when to stop and check. It keeps time, cost and quality under control and makes sure nothing important, like testing or training, gets forgotten.

WHEREWhy use it in software or systems development?

  • Software projects are complex, and without stages they get out of control fast.
  • Lots of people have to agree: the client, users, developers, testers and IT support.
  • Clients want to know the cost and the delivery date before they pay.
  • The decision points mean a bad project can be stopped before too much money is wasted.

2Stages

The five stages, in order

  1. 1Initiation
  2. 2Planning
  3. 3Execution
  4. 4Monitoring & control
  5. 5Closure

3Explanation of each stage

What happens at each stage

1 · Initiation

Deciding if the project is worth doing at all.

What happens

  • Someone spots a problem or an opportunity
  • A feasibility study checks if it's possible (technically, financially, legally)
  • A business case compares the costs with the benefits
  • The stakeholders are identified
  • A go / no-go decision is made

Who's involved

  • Sponsor or client
  • Project manager
  • Business analyst
  • Senior managers
  • Key stakeholders

What's produced

  • Business case
  • Feasibility report
  • Project charter or brief
  • Stakeholder list
2 · Planning

Turning the idea into a detailed plan that everyone agrees on.

What happens

  • Agree the scope and the requirements
  • Break the work into tasks (work breakdown structure)
  • Make the schedule, with milestones and the critical path
  • Set the budget and the resources
  • Plan for risks, quality and communication
  • The plan is signed off and becomes the baseline

Who's involved

  • Project manager
  • Business analyst
  • Team leaders
  • Client (signs off)
  • Finance

What's produced

  • Project plan (PID)
  • Requirements specification
  • Gantt chart
  • Budget
  • Risk register
  • Communication plan
3 · Execution

The team builds the product. In software projects this is where design, coding and testing happen.

What happens

  • Design, build and test the product
  • Give out tasks and hold team meetings
  • Buy equipment or software and work with suppliers
  • Keep the stakeholders updated
  • User acceptance testing near the end

Who's involved

  • Developers
  • Designers
  • Testers
  • Project manager
  • Suppliers
  • Users (for testing)

What's produced

  • The product (software, website, network…)
  • Test reports
  • Progress reports
  • User guides
4 · Monitoring & control

Runs alongside execution: checking the project is going to plan and fixing problems early.

What happens

  • Compare progress with the baseline (time, cost, quality)
  • Keep track of risks and issues
  • Change control: approve or reject changes
  • Quality checks and stage reviews
  • Take corrective action if we fall behind

Who's involved

  • Project manager
  • Sponsor / project board
  • Quality assurance
  • Team leaders

What's produced

  • Status reports
  • Updated plan and budget
  • Change log
  • Issue log
  • Updated risk register
5 · Closure

Handing over the finished product and officially ending the project.

What happens

  • The client tests and accepts the product (sign-off)
  • Hand over to the users and the support team, and train staff
  • Release the team and close any contracts
  • Final review: did we hit time, cost and quality?
  • Record the lessons learned and archive the documents

Who's involved

  • Project manager
  • Client or sponsor
  • Users
  • IT support team
  • Project team

What's produced

  • Signed acceptance form
  • End project report
  • Lessons learned report
  • Training materials

4Key features

How it works: key features

  • Stages in a fixed orderEach stage only starts when the one before it has been approved.
  • Stage gatesA go / no-go decision between stages. PRINCE2 calls these stage boundaries (AXELOS, 2017).
  • Deliverables at every stageEach stage produces something that can be checked and signed off.
  • A baseline planTime, cost and quality are agreed up front and used to measure progress.
  • Monitored all the way throughProgress is checked during the project, not just at the end.
  • TemporaryA clear start and a clear end, then the team moves on.

5Advantages

Advantages

+

Clear structure

Everyone knows which stage the project is in, what comes next and who is responsible for what.

Why it helps meI can plan my work stage by stage and explain progress to the client in a simple way.

+

Control of time and cost

There's a baseline plan to compare against, and progress is checked the whole way through.

Why it helps meI can spot early if we're behind or over budget, and fix it before it's too late.

+

Bad projects get stopped early

At every stage gate the project can be approved, changed or stopped.

Why it helps meI won't waste the client's money on something that clearly isn't going to work.

+

Clear deliverables and paperwork

Each stage produces documents or products that get signed off.

Why it helps meI have proof of what was agreed if there's an argument, and the handover at the end is easy.

6Disadvantages

Disadvantages

−

Hard to change later

The later a change is made, the more it costs (PMI, 2017).

How it could affect my projectIf the client changes their mind halfway through, it could delay the project and push the cost up.

−

Lots of paperwork

Business case, plans, reports, logs and sign-offs all take time to write.

How it could affect my projectOn a small project, writing documents could take time away from actually building the product.

−

Users see it late

Users are mostly involved at the start and at the end, not much in the middle.

How it could affect my projectIf I misunderstood a requirement, I might only find out at acceptance testing, when fixing it is expensive.

−

Only as good as the plan

If the estimates in planning are wrong, everything after them is affected.

How it could affect my projectOne bad estimate could make my whole schedule and budget unrealistic from day one.

7Suitable projects

What projects is it good for?

Best for projects where…

  • the goal and the requirements are clear and won't change much
  • the deadline and the budget are fixed
  • lots of people need to approve things
  • mistakes are expensive or dangerous (safety, the law, public money)

For example: installing a network, moving a company to a new system, or hospital and government IT.

  • Not ideal when the requirements keep changing, like a new app where you only find out what users want as you go. Agile suits that better (Beck et al., 2001).

Real-life example: NASA's Mars 2020 mission

NASA runs every mission through a formal life cycle, from Pre-Phase A (concept) to Phase F (closeout), with a Key Decision Point before each phase (NASA, 2016). The Perseverance rover launched on 30 July 2020 and landed on Mars on 18 February 2021 (NASA, no date).

Why it suits this project

  • Fixed deadline: Earth and Mars only line up for a launch about every 26 months. Miss it and you wait over two years.
  • No second chance: you can't repair a rover on Mars, so everything is designed, built and tested in order and checked at each gate.
  • Huge budget, many teams: formal approvals keep control of the money and the people.

8Link to my own project

Using it on my own project

My project: a click & collect website for Northgate Bakery, a small local bakery (made up for this unit). It has 8 weeks, a £9,900 budget and a team of four.

  1. 1InitiationMeet the owner, write a business case (lost phone orders, long queues, waste) and check it's possible in 8 weeks for £9,900. The owner says go.
  2. 2PlanningWrite the requirements with the owner, make the Gantt chart, budget and risk register, then get the plan signed off.
  3. 3ExecutionDesign, build and test the website with the team, with weekly check-ins and a demo for the owner.
  4. 4Monitoring & controlEvery Friday, compare progress with the Gantt chart and the budget. New ideas, like loyalty points, go through change control.
  5. 5Closure10 real test orders with the owner, staff training, hand over the logins and user guide, get it signed off and write the lessons learned.

Would it work well for my project?

Mostly, yes.

  • The deadline (before Christmas) and the budget are fixed
  • The requirements are fairly clear from the start
  • The owner wants to approve things at each step
  • The owner might change their mind once they see the design

So I'd keep the life cycle, but show the owner a clickable prototype during planning to get feedback early. That's a bit of Agile inside it.

9Conclusion

Conclusion

The project life cycle splits a project into five stages in a fixed order, initiation, planning, execution, monitoring & control and closure, with a decision point between them. It keeps a project under control from the first idea to the handover.

+Main strengths

  • A clear structure that everyone understands
  • Good control of time, cost and quality
  • Clear deliverables and sign-offs
  • Bad projects can be stopped early

−Main limitations

  • Changes are hard and expensive later on
  • A lot of paperwork
  • Users see the result quite late
  • It's only as good as the plan

When I think it should be used

When the goal is clear, the deadline and budget are fixed and approvals or safety matter, like my bakery website or a NASA mission. When the requirements will keep changing, I'd use Agile instead, or mix the two.

my opinion: the more you know at the start, the better it works
