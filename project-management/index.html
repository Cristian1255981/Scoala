02The basics

What is project management?

A project is a piece of work with a clear start and end, done to make something new, like a website, an app or a new network.

Project management is planning, organising and controlling that work so it finishes on time, on budget and to the right quality. Basically, it's making sure the thing actually gets done properly instead of just drifting.

The 5 stages of a project

  1. 1
    InitiationIs the idea worth doing? Business case, feasibility, who the client is.

  4. 5

03The basics

My example project: Northgate Bakery

To explain everything with a real example, I made up one project. I use it on every slide from now on.

Project briefv1.2 · signed off
Client
Northgate Bakery, a small family bakery with 6 staff (not a real business)
Problem
Phone orders get written on paper and some get lost. At busy times nobody can answer the phone, there's a queue out the door every Saturday, and unsold bread gets thrown away at closing time.
Product
A click & collect website. Customers choose what they want, pick a collection time and pay online. Staff see every order on a simple dashboard.
Deadline
8 weeks (40 working days), live before the Christmas orders start
Budget
£9,900, including £900 contingency
Team
Me (project manager), a designer who also builds the front end, a back-end developer and a part-time tester

Stakeholders

Everyone the project affects, or who can affect it.

  • Bakery ownerPays for it (the sponsor) and signs it off at the end.
  • Bakery staffWill use the orders dashboard every day.
  • CustomersWill order on their phones, so it has to be easy.
  • SuppliersThe payment provider and the hosting company. We depend on them.
  • Project teamMe, the designer, the back-end developer and the tester.

Why this project? It's small enough to plan properly, but it still has a budget, a deadline, customer data, payments and plenty of risks, so it covers the whole unit.

04Costs & time

Costs: building the budget

The budget is my estimate of what the project will cost. Once the owner approves it, it becomes the cost baseline, and at every review I compare what we've really spent against it.

Direct costs
Only exist because of this project, like the developers' days.
Indirect costs
Shared costs the bakery pays anyway, like electricity. They're not in my budget, but they're still real.
Contingency
Extra money kept aside in case risks happen. I used 10%.
Variance
The gap between planned and actual spending. If I planned £3,000 by week 4 but spent £3,300, that's £300 over.

How I estimated: for people it's days × day rate, and for everything else I used prices from suppliers' websites.

Milestone
A checkpoint with zero duration that shows real progress. Mine are: requirements signed off, design approved, payments working and the launch.
Deadline
The latest date something has to be finished. My launch deadline is the end of week 8.
Interim review
A planned check during the project (end of weeks 3 and 6). The owner and I compare progress with the plan and decide what to do.
Critical path
The longest chain of tasks that depend on each other. They have no spare time, so if one of them is late, the launch is late. Mine is A → B → E → G.

07Quality

Quality: working to standards

Finishing on time and on budget isn't enough. If the site is slow, buggy or hard to use, the project has still failed. Standards give me a clear idea of what "good quality" means.

ISO/IEC 25010: software product quality

An international model for judging software. The unit names the 2011 version (8 characteristics), and the newer 2023 version has 9. Click one to see what it means for the bakery site.

Functional suitability

Does it do the right things, correctly? Customers can order, pay and pick a time, and the total is always right.

W3C and WCAG 2.2: web standards

The W3C (World Wide Web Consortium) writes the standards the web runs on, like HTML and CSS. Its accessibility guidelines, WCAG 2.2, have four principles:

Perceivable
Alt text on product photos and good colour contrast.
Operable
Works with just a keyboard, buttons big enough to tap.
Understandable
Clear labels and errors, like "Please pick a collection time".
Robust
Valid HTML (checked with the W3C validator), so screen readers can use it.

There are 3 levels: A, AA and AAA. I'd aim for AA, which is what most UK organisations aim for.

This website tries to do it too: you can use it with just the keyboard, it has a dark mode, and the font (Atkinson Hyperlegible) was designed to be easy to read.

08Quality

Objectives, success criteria & requirements

My SMART objective

"By 30 November, launch a click & collect website where customers can order and pay online, within the £9,900 budget, and reach 50 online orders a week by the end of December, so fewer orders get lost and the Saturday queue gets shorter."

Specific: it says exactly what we're making and what it has to do.

Success criteria (how I'll know it worked)

  • Live by 30 November
  • Final cost within £9,900
  • Every "must have" requirement passes testing
  • Passes a WCAG 2.2 AA accessibility check
  • The owner signs it off after 10 real test orders

Functional or non-functional?

Functional = what the system does (its features). Non-functional = how well it does it (speed, security, accessibility) or rules it has to follow. Try sorting my requirements:

  1. Customers can choose a collection time.

  2. Every page loads in under 2 seconds on 4G.

  3. Staff can mark an order as ready, and the customer gets an email.

  4. The site meets WCAG 2.2 level AA.

  5. Customers can pay by card, Apple Pay or Google Pay.

  6. The site works on phones, tablets and laptops.

0 / 6 sorted

Tip: a good requirement can be tested. "Fast" can't, "under 2 seconds" can.

10Issues & benefits

Issues: dealing with problems as they happen

A risk is something that might happen. An issue is a problem that's happening now and needs action. These are the kinds of issue a project manager has to deal with:

Things outside the project can change halfway through: new laws, price rises, a supplier changing its service, new technology, or the economy.

I can't control any of these, but I can notice them early, work out what they mean for the plan and react before they turn into a bigger problem.

In my project

In week 4 the payment provider announced higher fees from January. I logged it as an issue, added the extra cost to my forecast and told the owner. We stayed with them, because switching would have delayed the launch.

  1. Measure

  3. Compare with the plan

  5. Find the cause

Every Friday I compare where we are with the baseline: tasks done, money spent and the quality of what's been built. When we're behind, there are two common fixes: crashing (adding more people or hours) and fast-tracking (doing tasks at the same time instead of one after another).

In my project

At review 1 (end of week 3) the design had finished 2 days late. Design is on the critical path, so the launch would have moved. I fast-tracked: the back-end developer had already started the payment set-up using the draft designs, so we got the 2 days back.

Different people need different information at different times, so I'd write a simple communication plan.

A good status update is short: what's done, what's late, what's next, and any decision I need.

Internal guidelines are the bakery's own rules, like brand colours, who signs things off and password rules. External guidelines are standards from outside, like WCAG, ISO/IEC 25010 and PCI DSS (the security standard for card payments). Then there's the law:

  • UK GDPR & Data Protection Act 2018Only collect the data we need, keep it safe and have a privacy policy.
  • Equality Act 2010Disabled customers can't be put at a disadvantage, so the site has to be accessible.
  • Computer Misuse Act 1990Getting into systems without permission is a crime, so we only test what we're allowed to test.
  • Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988Use our own photos, or images, fonts and code we have a licence for.
  • Consumer Contracts Regulations 2013Show clear prices and the total cost before the customer pays.
  • PECR 2003Ask before using non-essential cookies or sending marketing emails.
  • Food Information Regulations 2014Food sold online needs allergen information before the customer buys.

This is what I found in my research, not legal advice.

People on a project will disagree sometimes, about features, money, deadlines or who does what. Ignoring it only makes it worse. How I'd handle it:

  1. Listen to both sides
  2. Use facts, like the plan and the budget
  3. Look for a compromise
  4. If we still can't agree, the sponsor decides
  5. Write the decision down

In my project

In week 5 the owner asked for a loyalty points system. The developer said it would add 2 weeks and we'd miss the deadline. That's scope creep. I used change control: I wrote a change request showing the extra time and cost, we talked through the options and agreed to build it in "phase 2", after the launch.

Staff

New jobs, like checking the dashboard and packing orders. Some might worry about their jobs, so I'd involve them early and train them properly.

Other systems

The till and the stock count need to include online orders, and the site has to work with the payment provider and the email system.

Structures

Someone on the early shift becomes the "online orders lead", and there's a collection shelf by the door. The way the shop runs actually changes.

Growth

Operational

Project justification

The business case says the project is worth doing if the benefits are bigger than the costs and risks. I'd check this again at every review, and if it stopped being true, the project should change or stop.

Forecasting success

My forecast is based on guesses, so it's not a promise. Before starting I'd ask regular customers if they'd actually use it, and after the launch I'd compare the real numbers with the forecast.

12Risks

Risks: what could go wrong?

A risk is something uncertain that would affect the project if it happened. I can't get rid of every risk, but I can find them early, score them and plan for them.

Internal

Inside the project, so I have more control.

  • A developer gets ill
  • Estimates were too optimistic
  • Scope creep
  • Staff don't use the new system

External

Outside the project, so less control.

  • The payment provider changes
  • A cyber attack
  • The hosting goes down
  • A new law or a price rise

    13Risks

    Responding to risks

    Accept

    Do nothing now, just keep an eye on it. For small risks, or when preventing it would cost more than the damage.

    R6R7 Hosting outage, late photos

    Reduce

    Make it less likely to happen, or less bad if it does happen.

    R1R5 Start payments early, train the staff

    Contingency

    Have a plan B ready, with time or money set aside, that starts if the risk happens.

    R4 Freelance developer on standby

    Avoid

    Change the plan so the risk can't happen at all.

    R3 We never store card details

    Some books also mention transferring a risk (like insurance), but the unit uses these four.

    When a risk actually happens, it becomes an issue

    Write it in the issue log: what happened, when, and how bad it is.

    Week 5, MondayThe back-end developer is ill and off for 4 days. I log it as issue #3 (this was risk R4).
